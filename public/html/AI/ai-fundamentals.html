🤖 Artificial Intelligence

A practical map of AI, machine learning, deep learning, transformers, RAG, and agents — with the details you need to talk about the field accurately.

📚 Introduction to AI

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the field of building systems that perform tasks we associate with intelligence: recognizing patterns, making decisions, understanding language, perceiving the world, and improving from experience.

AI is not one algorithm. It is a toolbox. Early systems used hand-written rules. Modern systems mostly learn from data. The systems you use every day — search ranking, spam filters, photo tagging, chat assistants — are almost all narrow AI: excellent at a defined job, not a general mind.

AI is the broad field. Machine learning is one way to build AI. Deep learning is a kind of machine learning. Transformers power most LLMs; generative AI also includes diffusion models, not only chatbots.

What “intelligence” means in practice

  • Perception: see, hear, and read (images, speech, text)
  • Reasoning: combine facts, constraints, and goals
  • Learning: improve from examples or rewards instead of only rules
  • Language: parse, translate, summarize, and generate text
  • Action: recommend, control a robot, call a tool, or write code
Common mix-up: ChatGPT is not “general AI.” It is a very capable narrow language model. It can discuss many topics, but it does not have human-level understanding, persistent goals, or reliable world models. Fluency is not the same as AGI.

📅 History of AI

The field is older than neural networks. It moved through symbolic rules, statistical learning, the deep-learning boom, and then transformers. The dates below are the ones that actually changed how people build systems.

Year Milestone Why it matters
1950 Turing Test Alan Turing asks whether a machine can imitate a human in conversation
1956 Dartmouth Conference John McCarthy coins “Artificial Intelligence”; the field is named
1966 ELIZA Early chatbot; people over-attribute understanding to pattern matching
1997 Deep Blue IBM’s chess system beats Garry Kasparov — search + evaluation, not deep learning
2011 IBM Watson Wins Jeopardy!; retrieval and ranking at TV-quiz scale
2012 AlexNet CNNs crush ImageNet; GPUs + deep nets become the default for vision
2016 AlphaGo DeepMind beats Lee Sedol; reinforcement learning + search in Go
2017 Transformer “Attention Is All You Need” — parallel sequence models replace most RNNs
2018 BERT & GPT-1 Pretrained language models: encoder (BERT) vs decoder (GPT)
2020 GPT-3 175B parameters; in-context learning becomes a product idea
2022 ChatGPT Instruction-tuned + aligned GPT-3.5; conversational AI goes mainstream
2023 GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, Llama 2 Multimodal models, open weights, and a competitive frontier
2024 Reasoning models o1-style models spend tokens “thinking”; RAG and agents become default architecture
2025–2026 Agents & multimodal Tool use, computer-use agents, native audio/video, and cheaper open models

Two winters, then a boom

AI funding collapsed twice (roughly the 1970s and late 1980s) when rule-based systems failed to scale. The current wave is different because data, GPUs, and the transformer all arrived together. That does not mean AGI is solved — it means the engineering stack is finally productive.

🏗️ AI Hierarchical Structure

These two maps match the original notes file, with the wiring fixed. Keep two trees separate: capability (narrow vs general vs superintelligence) and technique (rules, ML, neural nets, generative models).

Is the original ASCII correct?

Mostly as a teaching sketch. Three places were misleading:

  • Deep Learning looked like a child of Unsupervised Learning because the vertical line sat under “Unsupervised.” DL is a technique (neural nets). You can do supervised CNNs, self-supervised LLMs, or RL with deep nets.
  • Generative AI hung only under Transformers. Text LLMs are transformers. Image models are often diffusion or GANs (sometimes with a transformer backbone).
  • “General AI encompasses multiple subfields” mixes AGI (a future capability) with the field of AI (which already has those subfields). Subfields live under Narrow AI today.

ChatGPT as “advanced Weak / Narrow AI” is correct. Calculator-as-AI is a stretch — a thermostat rule engine is a fairer “rule-based” example.

The three capability labels

  • Narrow AI (ANI): Where we are. One task or a cluster of related tasks — chess, transcription, coding assistance.
  • AGI: Hypothetical systems that can learn and transfer across most intellectual work at human level. Not achieved.
  • ASI: Speculative systems that exceed the best humans across most domains.

Don’t confuse AGI with “strong AI.” In philosophy, strong AI often meant a machine that is a mind. AGI is an engineering target: human-level capability.

📌 Key insights

  • The field of AI already has many subfields. AGI is a capability goal, not the parent of those subfields.
  • ML is a subset of AI, not a synonym. Data science overlaps; it is not a third intelligence level.
  • Deep learning is a subset of ML that uses neural networks — including supervised vision and self-supervised LLMs.
  • Progression of methods: rules → statistics → classical ML → deep learning → transformers → LLMs and diffusion.
  • RAG and agents are system patterns on top of generative models, not new branches of intelligence.

🎯 Types of AI

1. Narrow AI (Weak AI)

Designed for a defined task. It does not automatically transfer to a new domain. Almost every deployed system is narrow AI, including large chat models.

Examples:

  • Virtual assistants (Siri, Alexa)
  • Recommendation systems (Netflix, YouTube)
  • Image recognition and medical imaging models
  • Spam filters and fraud detectors
  • Code assistants and chat LLMs

2. General AI (AGI)

A system that can learn, reason, and apply knowledge across a wide range of tasks with human-level flexibility — including tasks it was not specialized for.

  • Status: Not achieved. Benchmarks keep moving; none equal “a competent human hire.”
  • Goal: Broad competence plus transfer, not just a bigger chatbot.

3. Superintelligent AI (ASI)

Theoretical AI that surpasses humans in most economically and scientifically valuable work, possibly including the ability to improve itself.

  • Status: Speculative. Useful as a risk and governance conversation, not as a product category.

Major branches of the field

Branch Description Typical applications
Machine Learning Learn patterns from data Fraud detection, recommendations, forecasting
Deep Learning Neural nets with many layers Vision, speech, language, protein folding
NLP Understand and generate language Search, translation, chat, summarization
Computer Vision Interpret images and video Face unlock, inspection, driving perception
Robotics Sense, plan, and act in the physical world Warehouse robots, drones, surgery
Planning & search Explore action sequences under constraints Games, routing, compilers, AlphaGo
Expert / rule systems Knowledge bases + inference rules Medical guidelines, business rules, tax engines

📊 Data Science

Data Science extracts insight from data using statistics, analysis, and often ML. AI needs data. Data science is how you get that data into a shape a model (or a human) can use.

Data types

Type Description Examples
Structured Rows and columns with a schema SQL tables, spreadsheets
Unstructured No fixed schema Text, images, audio, video
Semi-structured Nested fields, flexible schema JSON, XML, logs
Labeled Each example has a target Spam / not spam, house price
Unlabeled No target column Raw click logs, photo dumps

Preprocessing that actually matters

  1. Cleaning: missing values, duplicates, broken types, leaked test rows
  2. Transformation: scaling, encoding categoricals, log-transforms
  3. Integration: joins across product, billing, and support systems
  4. Reduction: feature selection, PCA, or just deleting unused columns
  5. Split discipline: train / validation / test, preferably by time for anything that drifts

AI vs ML vs DL vs data science

Concept Job Example
AI Make a system behave intelligently A routing agent that books a ticket
Machine Learning Learn the behavior from data A model that predicts churn
Deep Learning Learn with neural nets A vision model that finds tumors
Data Science Turn data into decisions and models A dashboard plus a forecast plus an A/B test

📈 Machine Learning

Machine Learning lets a system learn patterns from data instead of encoding every rule by hand. You choose a model family, feed it examples, and measure whether it generalizes to new examples.

The three classical settings: labeled targets, unlabeled structure, and learning from rewards.

1. Supervised learning

Each example is a pair (x, y). The model learns to predict y from x.

  • Classification: y is a class (spam, disease, intent)
  • Regression: y is a number (price, latency, remaining life)

Use cases: email spam, house prices, diagnosis from lab values, sentiment, credit risk

Workhorse models: linear / logistic regression, trees, random forests, gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM), small neural nets

2. Unsupervised learning

No labels. The algorithm finds structure: groups, axes, or outliers.

  • Clustering: K-means, hierarchical, DBSCAN — customer segments
  • Dimensionality reduction: PCA, t-SNE, UMAP — compress and visualize
  • Anomaly detection: “this row does not look like the others”

3. Semi-supervised learning

A little labeled data plus a lot of unlabeled data. Useful when labels are expensive (medical images, legal docs). The unlabeled mass helps the model learn the shape of the input space.

4. Self-supervised learning

Labels are created from the data itself. Predict the next word. Predict a masked patch of an image. This is how modern LLMs and many vision models are pretrained — you do not need humans to label every token.

5. Reinforcement learning

An agent takes actions in an environment, observes a new state, and receives a reward. It learns a policy that maximizes long-term reward.

Use cases: games (AlphaGo), robotics, bidding, some LLM alignment (RLHF)

Core ideas: exploration vs exploitation, value functions, policy gradients, reward hacking (the agent optimizes the metric, not your intent)

ML workflow

  1. Problem: what decision, for whom, what costs of being wrong
  2. Data collection: logs, labels, privacy constraints
  3. Preprocessing & features: the unglamorous 80%
  4. Baseline: majority class, last week’s average, a regex
  5. Model selection & training
  6. Evaluation: holdout, time split, slices, calibration
  7. Deployment: API, batch job, or on-device
  8. Monitoring & retraining: drift is normal
Leakage: if a feature is only known after the prediction time, or includes the label, your accuracy is fake. For anything that happens over days, split by time, not at random.

🧠 Deep Learning

Deep Learning is machine learning with neural networks that have many layers. Depth lets the model build a hierarchy of features: edges → parts → objects in vision; characters → words → syntax → meaning in language.

When deep learning wins

  • Unstructured data: images, audio, raw text
  • Enough data (thousands to billions of examples)
  • A GPU/TPU budget and a way to serve the model

For small tabular problems, gradient boosting still often beats a neural net. Start cheap.

Network families

Type Best for Examples
MLP / ANN Tabular patterns Churn, credit scoring baselines
CNN Grid-like data (images) ResNet, EfficientNet, YOLO
RNN / LSTM / GRU Sequences (older default) Early translation, time series
Transformer Language, vision, audio, multimodal BERT, GPT, ViT, Whisper
GNN Graphs (molecules, social nets) Drug discovery, recommendations

🔗 Neural Networks

A neural network is a stack of linear maps and nonlinearities. Each connection has a weight. Each unit has a bias. Training is just adjusting those numbers so the loss goes down.

Data flows left to right. Learning changes the weights. The activation function is what makes the stack able to fit nonlinear patterns.

The neuron

Formula: output = activation(Σ (weight × input) + bias)

Without an activation, stacked layers collapse into one linear map. ReLU, GELU, and SiLU are the usual hidden-layer choices in modern nets.

Layers

  • Input: raw features or embeddings
  • Hidden: learned representations (more layers = deeper)
  • Output: logits, probabilities, or generated tokens

Activation functions

Function Range Typical use
Sigmoid (0, 1) Binary output (less common in hidden layers)
Tanh (−1, 1) Older hidden layers; zero-centered
ReLU [0, ∞) Default hidden activation; fast
GELU / SiLU smooth ReLU-like Transformers (GPT, BERT-style)
Softmax probabilities that sum to 1 Multi-class or next-token heads

CNNs in one paragraph

A convolution slides a small filter over the image. Early filters learn edges; later layers learn objects. Pooling downsamples. Skip connections (ResNet) let gradients flow through very deep stacks. YOLO-style detectors predict boxes in one pass for real-time use.

RNNs in one paragraph

RNNs step through a sequence and keep a hidden state. They struggle with long-range dependencies (vanishing gradients). LSTM and GRU add gates. Transformers largely replaced them for language because attention is parallel and sees the whole context at once.

📊 Training & Evaluation

One iteration: predict, measure error, send gradients backward, step the optimizer. An epoch is one full pass over the dataset.

Forward pass and backpropagation

  1. A batch of examples goes through the network
  2. The last layer produces predictions
  3. A loss compares predictions to labels (cross-entropy, MSE, …)
  4. Backprop uses the chain rule to get ∂loss/∂weight for every weight
  5. An optimizer (SGD, Adam, AdamW) takes a step of size learning rate

Vocabulary

Term Meaning
Epoch One full pass through the training set
Batch size Examples per update (32–4096+ depending on hardware)
Iteration / step One weight update
Learning rate Step size. Too high = diverge. Too low = crawl.
Scheduler Changes LR over time (warmup, cosine decay)

Classification metrics

Metric Formula Use when
Accuracy (TP + TN) / N Classes are balanced and errors cost the same (rare)
Precision TP / (TP + FP) False positives are expensive
Recall TP / (TP + FN) False negatives are expensive
F1 Harmonic mean of precision and recall Need one number on imbalanced data
ROC-AUC / PR-AUC Ranking quality PR-AUC when positives are rare

Regression metrics

Metric Notes
MAE Average absolute error — easy to explain
MSE / RMSE Punish large errors; RMSE is in the target’s units
R² Variance explained — can look great while MAE is still useless

Underfitting vs overfitting

Underfit = too simple (high bias). Overfit = memorizes noise (high variance). You want low training error and low validation error.
Aspect Underfitting Good fit Overfitting
Train error High Low Very low
Val error High Low High
Fix Bigger model, better features, train longer — Regularization, more data, simpler model, early stopping

Regularization

  • L2 (weight decay): keep weights small — default in AdamW
  • L1: sparse weights, crude feature selection
  • Dropout: randomly drop units during training
  • Early stopping: halt when validation loss stops improving
  • Data augmentation: extra views of the same example
  • Batch / layer / RMS norm: stabilize activations so training does not explode

💬 Natural Language Processing

NLP is how machines read, interpret, and produce human language. Today that mostly means transformers plus tokenization. Classical NLP (regex, TF-IDF, CRFs) is still useful for cheap, tight tasks.

Evolution

  1. Rules: grammars and pattern lists
  2. Statistics: n-grams, HMMs, noisy channel translation
  3. Classical ML: bag-of-words + logistic regression / SVMs
  4. Deep learning: word2vec, RNNs, seq2seq
  5. Transformers & LLMs: pretrained on the internet, then aligned

Core tasks

Task What you want Example
Classification A label for a document Spam, intent, topic
NER Spans: people, orgs, dates Extract vendors from invoices
Sentiment Tone or aspect sentiment Review mining
Translation Same meaning, new language Product localization
QA / RAG Answer grounded in sources Internal docs chatbot
Summarization Shorter text, same facts Meeting notes
Generation New text or code Drafts, copilots

Embeddings

An embedding is a vector that puts similar meanings nearby in space. Older static embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText) gave each word one vector. Contextual models (BERT, sentence transformers, LLM embedding APIs) give a vector that depends on the sentence. That is the substrate for search, clustering, and RAG.

🔎 Attention & Transformers

The transformer is the architecture behind nearly every modern LLM and many vision/audio models. The idea: every token can look at every other token in the sequence, in parallel.

Self-attention builds a weighted mix of other tokens. Multi-head attention runs several of those mixes at once. Positional encoding injects word order.

Pieces you should be able to name

  • Tokenization: text → subword IDs (BPE, SentencePiece). “unhappiness” might become un + happiness.
  • Embeddings: each token ID becomes a vector.
  • Positional information: sin/cos encodings or rotary (RoPE) so order is not lost.
  • Query / Key / Value: attention scores come from Q·K, then those weights mix V.
  • Multi-head: several attention patterns in parallel (syntax vs coreference, etc.).
  • Feed-forward / MLP: per-token nonlinear transform after attention.
  • Residuals + layer norm: keep deep stacks trainable.

Encoder vs decoder vs encoder-decoder

Family Masking Famous models Good at
Encoder Bidirectional (see both sides) BERT, RoBERTa Classification, NER, embeddings
Decoder Causal (only past tokens) GPT, Llama, Claude, Gemini Generation, chat, code
Encoder–decoder Both T5, BART, original Transformer Translation, summarization
Why transformers beat RNNs: they train in parallel on GPUs, they have a direct path between distant tokens, and they scale predictably as you add data and parameters.

🤖 Large Language Models

LLMs are decoder (or encoder-decoder) transformers trained on huge text corpora to predict the next token. After pretraining they are usually instruction-tuned and aligned so they follow requests instead of only completing internet text.

Pretrain for knowledge and fluency. SFT for following instructions. RLHF / DPO for preferred style, safety, and helpfulness. None of this guarantees factuality.

Training vs inference

Training Inference
Goal Set the weights Use the weights

What LLMs are bad at (unless you add systems around them)

  • Fresh facts: weights are a snapshot. Use RAG or tools.
  • Arithmetic & exact tables: they approximate. Use a calculator or DB.
  • Hallucination: fluent wrong answers. Ground, cite, and evaluate.
  • Long, precise procedures: they drift. Use agents with checks, or don’t.

🎨 Generative AI

Generative AI produces new samples that look like the training distribution: text, images, audio, video, 3D, or code. LLMs are one family. Images are dominated by diffusion today. Older work used GANs and VAEs.

Diffusion: destroy the image with noise, then train a net to reverse the noise. Text (and now video) conditions each step.

Techniques

Technique Idea Examples
Autoregressive LLMs Next-token prediction GPT, Claude, Llama, Gemini
Diffusion Iterative denoising Stable Diffusion, DALL·E, Sora-class video
GANs Generator vs discriminator game StyleGAN; less used for text-to-image now
VAEs Encode to a latent, decode back Compression, some image stacks
NeRF / 3D gens Scene or mesh from views / text Novel-view synthesis, game assets
Multimodal One model, many media GPT-4o, Gemini, vision-language models

Mixture of Experts (MoE)

Instead of using every parameter on every token, an MoE model routes each token to a few expert MLPs. You get a large parameter count with smaller compute per token (Mixtral-style). Routing and load-balancing are the hard parts.

👁️ Computer Vision

Computer Vision turns pixels into structure: labels, boxes, masks, pose, text, or 3D. CNNs were the workhorse after 2012. Vision transformers (ViT) and multimodal LLMs now share that job.

Same image, four jobs: one label, boxes, per-pixel classes, and a skeleton. Always name the task before you pick a model.

Tasks

Task Output Where it shows up
Classification One or more labels Moderation, cataloging
Detection Boxes + classes Driving, CCTV, retail shelves
Segmentation Class (or instance) per pixel Medical imaging, AR, maps
Pose Keypoints Fitness, animation, sports
OCR Text from pixels IDs, invoices, signs
Depth / 3D Geometry Robotics, SLAM, reconstruction

Model lineages worth knowing

  • Classification CNNs: VGG, ResNet, EfficientNet
  • Detectors: R-CNN family (two-stage), YOLO (single-stage, real-time)
  • ViT / CLIP: patches + transformers; CLIP ties images to language for zero-shot labels and search
  • Generative vision: GANs historically; diffusion now; VLMs that talk about images

📚 RAG — Retrieval-Augmented Generation

An LLM’s weights are a frozen encyclopedia. RAG fetches relevant documents at query time and stuffs them into the prompt so the model can answer with your facts, not just memorized internet text.

Embed → retrieve top-k chunks → generate from question + context. Citations are how you debug the system when it is wrong.

Pipeline

  1. Ingest: split docs into chunks (too big = noisy; too small = no context)
  2. Embed & index: store vectors in a vector DB (pgvector, Chroma, Pinecone, Weaviate)
  3. Retrieve: dense search, often plus keyword (hybrid) and reranking
  4. Generate: LLM sees question + passages; instructed to say “I don’t know” if unsupported
  5. Evaluate: faithfulness (grounded?), relevance, citation precision

Why RAG instead of only fine-tuning?

  • Docs change daily — retrieval updates without retraining
  • You can cite sources and inspect what the model saw
  • Fine-tuning is for style and skills; RAG is for knowledge
Failure modes: bad chunking, wrong embedding model, retrieving similar-but-wrong passages, stuffing too much context, and never measuring hallucinations. RAG is a search problem wearing a chatbot costume.

🧭 AI Agents

A chatbot answers in one shot. An agent loops: think, pick a tool, observe the result, repeat until the goal is done or it should stop. That is how “book a flight,” “fix this repo,” or “file these invoices” get automated.

Perceive → plan → act with tools → observe. Guardrails, budgets, and human approval matter more than a clever prompt.

Building blocks

Piece Role
Policy (LLM) Chooses the next action from the transcript
Tools APIs: search, SQL, calendar, code exec, browser
Memory Short-term = context window; long-term = DB / RAG
Planner Optional: break a goal into steps before acting
Orchestration Timeouts, max steps, retries, human-in-the-loop

Patterns

  • Tool-calling / function calling: model emits a structured call; your runtime executes it
  • ReAct: explicit Thought → Action → Observation traces
  • Multi-agent: specialist roles (researcher, coder, critic) with a supervisor
  • Computer use: the agent operates a GUI like a human — powerful and easy to misuse
Agents fail in production when tools are unconstrained, loops never terminate, or the model is allowed to invent APIs. Cap steps, log every tool call, and require confirmation for anything that spends money or changes data.

🎛️ Fine-Tuning & PEFT

Pretraining teaches general language. Fine-tuning specializes the model: your tone, your JSON schema, your medical jargon. You rarely train all weights of a 70B model from scratch.

Method What changes When to use it
Full fine-tune Every parameter You have lots of data and GPUs
LoRA / QLoRA Small adapter matrices (QLoRA = quantized base) Default PEFT; cheap, swappable adapters
Prefix / prompt tuning Learned virtual tokens Tiny data, keep base frozen
SFT Supervised instruction pairs Teach a format or skill
DPO / RLHF Preference data Style, safety, “which answer is better”
Rule of thumb: prompt well first, then RAG for knowledge, then fine-tune for consistent behavior. Fine-tuning will not magically add last week’s wiki.

✍️ Prompting & Sampling

A prompt is the entire context the model conditions on: system instructions, retrieved docs, few-shot examples, and the user message. Sampling parameters decide how greedy or diverse the next token is.

Prompting patterns that hold up

  • Clear role + constraints: who it is, what format, what it must not do
  • Few-shot: 2–5 input/output examples beat adjectives like “be professional”
  • Chain of thought: ask for steps when the task is multi-hop (or use a reasoning model)
  • Structured output: JSON schema / tool calls instead of free prose
  • Separation: instructions vs untrusted user/retrieved text (prompt injection lives here)

Sampling knobs

Knob Effect
Temperature 0 ≈ greedy/deterministic; higher ≈ more random
Top-p (nucleus) Sample from the smallest set of tokens whose probability ≥ p
Max tokens Hard cap on output length
Stop sequences End generation at a delimiter

Reasoning models

Models trained to spend tokens on an internal chain of thought (o1-class and successors) often beat a single-shot GPT-style answer on math, code, and planning. You pay with latency and cost. Use them when the extra thinking is cheaper than a wrong action.

🚀 More Advanced Ideas

Transfer learning

Start from a model trained on a huge dataset (ImageNet, web text). Replace or add a small head, freeze early layers if data is scarce, then fine-tune. You inherit features you could never learn from 500 of your own photos.

Foundation models

A foundation model is trained at scale on broad data and adapted to many tasks (via prompts, adapters, or heads). GPT, Llama, Whisper, and CLIP are in this category. The product is the adaptation layer, not a new net from scratch.

Ensemble methods (classical ML)

Method How Example
Bagging Average models trained on bootstrap samples Random Forest
Boosting Each model fixes the previous residual XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost
Stacking A meta-model learns to combine base models Kaggle blends

Hyperparameter search

  • Grid: exhaustive, slow
  • Random: often better per hour than grid
  • Bayesian / Hyperband: spend trials where they help
  • AutoML: useful baselines; still needs a human on leakage and metrics

Compression for serving

Technique Idea Win
Quantization FP32 → INT8 / INT4 Smaller VRAM, faster decode
Pruning Drop unused weights Sparsity (hardware must support it)
Distillation Student copies a teacher Small model, similar quality
Speculative decoding Draft with a small model, verify with a large one Lower latency

Federated learning & privacy

Train across devices without pooling raw data (phones send gradients, not photos). Combine with differential privacy if you need mathematical privacy budgets. Harder to debug; used when data cannot leave the device or tenant.

⚙️ MLOps

MLOps is how models survive after the notebook: versioning, tests, deployment, monitoring, and retraining. LLMOps adds evals, prompt/version control, tracing, and cost/latency budgets.

Lifecycle

  1. Define the decision and the metric
  2. Collect and document data (lineage)
  3. Train and track experiments
  4. Evaluate offline (and with humans for LLMs)
  5. Register the model / prompt / index
  6. Deploy (API, batch, edge)
  7. Monitor quality, drift, cost, and abuse
  8. Retrain or refresh retrieval when the world moves

  • Shadow: new model scores live traffic, users still see the old one
  • Canary: 1–5% of users first
  • Blue-green: two environments, flip the pointer
  • A/B: measure the business metric, not only loss

⚖️ AI Ethics & Responsible AI

Capability without process becomes bias, leaks, and unaccountable decisions. Responsible AI is product work: data, evals, access control, and a human who owns outcomes.

Bias and fairness

  • Historical, representation, measurement, and amplification biases
  • Mitigations: better data, slice metrics, fairness constraints, audits — not a single “debias” button

Privacy

  • Training data can be regurgitated; prompts can contain secrets
  • Techniques: minimization, anonymization, federated learning, differential privacy, tenant isolation

Explainability

  • Trees and linear models are inspectable; deep nets need probes (SHAP, LIME, attention — none are perfect)
  • High-stakes domains (lending, medicine) often require a simpler model or a documented override

Security

  • Adversarial examples: tiny input changes that flip a classifier
  • Prompt injection / jailbreaks: untrusted text that hijacks an agent
  • Data poisoning: bad training or RAG documents
  • Model theft: distillation of APIs

Principles to operationalize

Principle In practice
Fairness Measure harm by group; do not ship on average accuracy alone
Safety Refuse dangerous actions; rate-limit tools
Privacy Do not train on secrets; redact logs
Transparency Tell users when they talk to a model; cite sources
Accountability A named owner, an incident process, audit trails
Human control Augment people; keep a stop button for irreversible acts

🌍 Applications

Healthcare

  • Imaging: CXR, CT, MRI, retinal photos — assist, do not silently replace clinicians
  • Discovery: AlphaFold-style structure, molecule generation, trial matching
  • Operations: coding, prior auth, ambient clinical notes — high hallucination risk

Finance

  • Fraud, AML, and account takeover — class imbalance is the whole game
  • Credit decisioning — fairness and explainability are legal requirements, not extras
  • Document intelligence and report drafting with RAG over filings

Software & knowledge work

  • Code completion, test generation, incident summarization
  • Internal search (RAG) over wikis, tickets, and runbooks
  • Agents that open PRs — review like you would a junior hire

Physical world

  • Autonomy: perception + prediction + planning; the hard part is the tail of rare events
  • Manufacturing: visual inspection, predictive maintenance, scheduling
  • Agriculture & climate: crop stress, yield, satellite deforestation, grid optimization
